# EEA Positioning & Voice

**Status:** Authoritative editorial guidance  
**Applies to:** Enterprise Ethereum Alliance website, Ethereum Intelligence, research, events, membership communications, social content, presentations, AI-generated copy and contributor work.

This document defines how the Enterprise Ethereum Alliance describes itself, its work and Ethereum.

When new copy conflicts with this document, this document takes precedence unless the positioning is intentionally updated here.

## 1. What EEA is

The Enterprise Ethereum Alliance (EEA) is the **member-led nonprofit connecting institutions to Ethereum through intelligence, practitioners, open standards and institutional peers.**

EEA sits between Ethereum's open ecosystem and the organizations trying to understand, implement or participate in it.

EEA helps institutions:

- understand what is changing;
- determine what those changes mean for their organization;
- work through implementation questions with experienced practitioners;
- connect with peers facing similar decisions;
- bring institutional requirements into relevant conversations, standards and initiatives.

A useful short expression is:

**The institutional interface to Ethereum.**

This is a positioning line, not something that needs to be repeated mechanically on every page.

## 2. What EEA is not

EEA is not:

- the Ethereum Foundation;
- the governing body of Ethereum;
- the organization setting Ethereum's roadmap;
- a protocol vendor;
- a consulting firm;
- an investment adviser;
- a lobbying organization;
- a generic crypto trade association;
- a marketing organization whose role is to tell institutions that Ethereum is good.

EEA's credibility comes from helping institutions understand Ethereum accurately enough to make their own decisions.

We explain, connect, convene and help translate.

We do not manufacture certainty or imply control over an open ecosystem.

## 3. The central idea

EEA should increasingly be associated with one territory:

### Institutional Ethereum

This includes the questions organizations face when Ethereum moves from an abstract technology into real financial and enterprise infrastructure.

Examples include:

- protocol changes and network upgrades;
- tokenization;
- payments and settlement;
- custody and wallets;
- privacy;
- interoperability;
- L1 and L2 architecture;
- security;
- infrastructure readiness;
- operational controls;
- standards;
- regulation and policy;
- implementation requirements.

EEA should become one of the places institutions look when asking:

> What is changing in Ethereum, why does it matter to us, and who should we be talking to?

## 4. Messaging hierarchy

When describing EEA, use this hierarchy.

### Level 1 — Positioning

**The Enterprise Ethereum Alliance is the member-led nonprofit connecting institutions to Ethereum.**

### Level 2 — How

Through:

**Intelligence. Practitioners. Open standards. Institutional peers.**

### Level 3 — Outcome

EEA helps teams:

**Understand what is changing. Work through what it means. Connect with the people doing it.**

### Level 4 — Participation

Institutions can use EEA to:

- learn;
- ask questions;
- compare approaches;
- access practitioners;
- participate in working groups;
- contribute requirements;
- attend institutional forums;
- engage with the Ethereum ecosystem.

## 5. Voice

EEA should sound:

**Institutional. Informed. Neutral. Close to the work.**

The reader should feel that EEA has access to the people actually working on the problem, understands the technical substance and can explain it without unnecessary complexity.

The voice is confident without being promotional.

It is sophisticated without sounding academic.

It is technically credible without trying to prove its technical credibility through jargon.

## 6. Writing principles

### Concrete over promotional

Prefer:

> Private briefings with Ethereum protocol practitioners.

Not:

> Unparalleled access to leading blockchain experts.

Prefer:

> Understand how upcoming Ethereum changes could affect wallet architecture.

Not:

> Stay ahead of the rapidly evolving blockchain landscape.

Evidence should create credibility. Adjectives should not have to.

### Implications over announcements

EEA is most useful when it explains consequences.

Do not stop at:

> A new EIP has been proposed.

Ask:

> What could this change for custody, wallets, infrastructure, controls, cost or operations?

The EEA layer is often the translation between technical development and institutional consequence.

### Primary sources over commentary

Whenever possible, start from:

- EIPs;
- Ethereum developer discussions;
- specifications;
- standards;
- regulatory documents;
- institutional publications;
- direct practitioner input.

Clearly distinguish:

**What the source says**  
from  
**EEA's interpretation of what it may mean.**

Do not turn interpretation into fact.

### Accuracy over advocacy

Do not write as though EEA's job is to prove Ethereum wins.

Avoid language such as:

- best blockchain;
- Ethereum is the future;
- inevitable;
- revolutionary;
- game-changing;
- world-leading;
- premier;
- official Ethereum organization;
- definitive solution.

Explain the tradeoffs and let the substance do the work.

### Institutional language over crypto marketing

Use terms that a CTO, product head, architect, operations lead or digital-asset executive would recognize.

Avoid unnecessary crypto-native shorthand when clearer language exists.

At the same time, do not hide legitimate technical terminology when it is necessary to understand the issue.

Explain it.

Do not dumb it down.

## 7. Ethereum Intelligence

Ethereum Intelligence is EEA's flagship institutional intelligence program.

Its role is to translate developments in Ethereum into information that institutional teams can use.

A strong Intelligence piece should answer:

### What changed?

State the development plainly.

### Why does it matter?

Explain the consequence rather than repeating the technical change.

### Who may be affected?

For example:

- custody teams;
- wallet providers;
- infrastructure teams;
- product teams;
- compliance;
- risk;
- operations;
- L2 operators;
- validators.

### When does it matter?

Distinguish:

- early proposal;
- active discussion;
- likely upcoming change;
- testnet;
- scheduled upgrade;
- production relevance.

Avoid creating false urgency.

### What should institutions watch?

Identify decisions, dependencies, implementation considerations or provider readiness questions.

### Primary sources

Give the reader a path back to authoritative material.

## 8. Website copy

The EEA website should answer questions, not behave like an association brochure.

Every important page should make it obvious:

**What is this?**  
**Who is it for?**  
**Why would I care?**  
**What can I do next?**

Brand headlines can be editorial.

Supporting copy should be explicit.

Example:

### Headline

**Where institutions shape their Ethereum strategy.**

### Supporting copy

EEA connects institutions with Ethereum practitioners, intelligence, open standards and peers working through the same implementation decisions.

Do not require the reader — or a search engine — to infer what EEA actually does from a clever headline.

## 9. Membership

Membership should be described in terms of **access and participation**, not association status.

The useful question is not:

> Why should I support EEA?

It is:

> What does my team actually get?

Examples:

- access to Ethereum intelligence;
- private briefings;
- practitioner access;
- institutional peers;
- forums;
- working groups;
- implementation discussions;
- standards work;
- opportunities to bring institutional requirements into the ecosystem.

Membership copy should remain practical.

Avoid generic language about "joining a global community of innovators."

## 10. EEA and Ethereum

Be precise about the relationship.

EEA participates in the Ethereum ecosystem.

EEA works with Ethereum practitioners and organizations.

EEA provides an environment in which institutional requirements can be discussed with people building Ethereum.

EEA does not control Ethereum.

EEA does not speak for Ethereum.

EEA membership by an Ethereum organization does not imply that EEA controls or represents that organization.

Similarly, EEA analysis of an EIP does not constitute endorsement of that EIP.

Avoid using these broad terms when a more precise description is available.

## 12. Content test

Before publishing EEA content, ask:

**Is this saying something useful, or simply saying that EEA exists?**

**Could a knowledgeable institutional reader learn something concrete?**

**Are we distinguishing facts from interpretation?**

**Can the important claim be traced back to evidence or a primary source?**

**Does this sound like someone close to the work rather than a marketing department?**

**Would we still publish this sentence if we removed every adjective?**

If not, rewrite it.

## 13. AI and contributor instructions

Anyone — human or AI — producing EEA material should follow these rules.

Do not:

- invent facts, metrics, endorsements or relationships;
- imply EEA controls Ethereum;
- describe EEA as the official representative of Ethereum;
- manufacture urgency;
- keyword-stuff copy for SEO;
- write generic association language;
- add marketing superlatives without evidence;
- make technical material complicated simply to sound authoritative;
- broadly rewrite existing copy when a precise edit is sufficient.

Do:

- preserve factual nuance;
- prefer primary sources;
- explain institutional implications;
- identify uncertainty;
- make clear when something is proposed versus deployed;
- use explicit terminology around what EEA actually does;
- connect related EEA knowledge rather than producing isolated content;
- optimize first for a sophisticated human reader and secondarily for machines.

## 14. The standard

A strong piece of EEA communication should feel like it came from an organization that:

**knows the people, understands the technology, understands institutional constraints and does not need to exaggerate its importance.**

That is the EEA voice.
